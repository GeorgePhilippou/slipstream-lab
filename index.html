What's being solved

A lattice Boltzmann solver, a standard CFD method that reproduces the Navier–Stokes equations, updates every grid cell many times a second. Walls are no-slip. Lift and drag are measured from the momentum the air gives the wing.

How the ice grows

Each droplet has mass and inertia, with standard sphere drag (Schiller–Naumann). Small droplets follow the air around the nose; big ones can't turn and strike further back. A freezing fraction decides how much freezes on impact. The rest runs back along the surface. Frozen cells become solid wall, so the airflow then has to go around the ice.

Limits

This is 2D, at Reynolds numbers far below a real wing's (about 10⁷). Ice is built from grid cells about 2 cm across. The heat balance is simplified to a temperature-based freezing fraction. Expect the right trends: rime versus glaze shapes, lost lift and extra drag. The exact numbers won't match.

The method

The vortex-lattice method is the workhorse of early aircraft design, used in tools such as AVL and XFLR5. The wing and tail are covered in horseshoe vortices. Their strengths are solved together so that no air passes through any panel. Lift and pitching moment come from Kutta–Joukowski. Induced drag comes from a Trefftz-plane analysis of the wake. Checked here against theory: a rectangular wing of aspect ratio 6 gives a lift slope of 4.29/rad and a span efficiency of 0.96.

What's estimated

Profile drag uses turbulent skin friction with a thickness form factor, plus the fuselage and fin, the standard textbook build-up. Stall uses the critical-section method: the wing stalls when its most heavily loaded section reaches that airfoil's maximum lift. Section maximum lift is a fit to published NACA data at high Reynolds number.

Limits

This is potential flow: no viscosity in the lift, no compressibility (stay below about Mach 0.5), and no vortex lift on sharp deltas. The fuselage adds drag but no lift or pitching moment, and the fin isn't modelled for sideslip. Treat absolute numbers as within about 10%; comparisons between designs are much more reliable.

    The physics

    The atmosphere follows the International Standard Atmosphere. Lift = ½ρV²S·CL. The finite wing's lift slope uses Prandtl's lifting-line correction, and induced drag is CL²/(πeAR). Above the critical Mach number, wave drag rises with Lock's fourth-power law. The point-mass equations of motion are integrated 50 times per simulated second.

    How ice hurts

    Ice grows at β·LWC·V/ρice. The collection efficiency β comes from the Langmuir–Blodgett droplet-impact fit. The loss of maximum lift and the extra drag scale with √(ice height ÷ chord), following trends reported in icing wind-tunnel studies. Glaze horns hurt more than rime.

    Why icing is a mid-altitude danger

    Cloud droplets stay liquid well below 0 °C, but colder than about −20 °C most clouds are ice crystals that don't stick. A jet cruising at 11 km (−56 °C) rarely picks up airframe ice. Turboprops and light aircraft fly at the altitudes where it forms.

    A real 3D solver

    This is a D3Q19 lattice Boltzmann solver with a Smagorinsky turbulence model, running as WebGPU compute shaders. Only one half of the model is simulated. A mirror boundary at the centreline doubles the resolution, which is standard practice in CFD.

    What to look for

    The wing pushes high-pressure air outward underneath and low-pressure air inward on top. At the tips the two meet and roll up into vortices. Slice the flow behind the wing in Spin mode to see them. They tilt the airflow down (downwash), which cuts lift and adds induced drag. This is why long, slender wings are more efficient.

    Limits

    The grid is about 1 million cells, so a wing chord spans roughly 10–20 cells and Reynolds numbers stay in the low thousands. The real models are drawn smooth, but the solver sees them as voxels; switch on "Show what the solver sees" to compare. Ice thinner than one cell won't show in 3D.
